# Voice and Tone

**Project:** Tajima Ramen
**Last updated:** 2026-07-15

## Purpose

This file defines the brand's writing voice for AI-assisted content production. It is the authority on copy. If a piece of writing is not consistent with this document, it gets revised, not argued about.

**Content provenance for this build:** agency-produced, AI-assisted, human-approved. Every line ships through a human. Nothing generated here goes live unread.

---

## The voice in one comparison

**On brand:**
> Sam Morikizono grew up in Amagasaki. He's been making ramen in San Diego since 2001. The noodles are now his too.

**Off brand:**
> At Tajima, we elevate the timeless craft of Japanese ramen with curated ingredients and an immersive dining journey.

The distance between those two sentences is the entire project. When a copy decision is ambiguous, move toward the first one.

---

## Brand Voice Principles

### Core voice attributes

- **Earned.** Twenty-five years of one owner in one city. The brand does not need to convince anyone. It needs to show its work and let the reader draw the conclusion. Confidence without ornament.
- **Specific.** Amagasaki, not "Japan." Tonkotsu, not "rich broth." Two years, not "a long time." A machine imported from Japan, not "state-of-the-art equipment." Specificity is the entire difference between craft and category.
- **Warm.** Twenty-five years of regulars and a staff that gets named in reviews more than the food does. The welcome is real and it is underneath everything, even when the copy is about a noodle machine. Warm is not folksy and it is not chatty.

### Tone guidelines

**Plainspoken, not casual.** Semi-formal. Reads like a skilled cook explaining what he just made, in short sentences, without selling you on it. Not a press release, not a text message.

**Culturally specific, not culturally decorative.** Japanese words get used for things that have Japanese names: dishes, regions, techniques, ingredients. Tonkotsu, chashu, takenoko, izakaya, karaage, Amagasaki. Japanese words do not get used for feelings. No shokunin, no ichigo ichie, no omotenashi, no kaizen. Reaching for Japanese poetry is how a brand with a real Japanese founder ends up sounding like a brand that visited once.

**Never nostalgic about the past, never apologetic about it.** The story is what Tajima built, not what it fixed. The previous noodles were not bad. The new ones are ours. That distinction is load-bearing.

---

## The five rules

1. **Show the work.** "Broth simmered every morning and driven to every location" beats "lovingly crafted broth." Specifics travel. Abstractions do not.
2. **Name things.** Places, people, dishes, techniques, numbers. Every named thing is a credibility deposit.
3. **Stay short.** Long sentences read as marketing. Short sentences read as conviction. If a sentence has a subordinate clause, look at it twice.
4. **Don't reach for Japanese poetry.** See above.
5. **Sound like a person, not a brand.** "Sam built this room because he was tired of the noodles he was buying." Not "Tajima is proud to announce the launch of an exciting new noodle production initiative."

## Writing Rules

### Always

- Write toward the positioning sentence: *San Diego's craft Japanese ramen house, founder-built since 2001, now making its own noodles.* It never appears verbatim on the site. Every page is written toward it anyway.
- Use concrete nouns and real numbers. If a claim cannot be pointed at, cut it.
- Put the proof next to the claim, in the same breath. Not in a callout box three scrolls down.
- Use active voice with a human subject. Sam built it. The crew makes it. We simmer it.
- Vary sentence length deliberately. Short. Short. Then one that carries a little more weight because it earned the room.
- Check every claim against the Claims Inventory below before writing it.

### Never

- Never use em dashes. Use commas, periods, parentheses. Hard rule, applies to copy, docs, code comments, alt text, meta descriptions, everything.
- Never use banned filler:
  - Generic: "Unlock your potential," "Leverage our expertise," "Cutting-edge solutions," "Seamless experience," "Game-changing"
  - Brand-specific: elevated, curated, immersive, culinary journey, authentic (as a claim), passion, passionate about, dive into, crafted with love, truly, truly authentic, a feast for the senses, our story began, we are passionate about, nestled, boasts, whether you're a X or a Y, more than just a Z, in today's world, at the end of the day, it's no secret that
  - Retired 2025 voice: "All Slurps Welcome," "Come Get Some Slizzurp," "I'm Late for Ramen"
- Never claim expertise or credentials that are not verified. See Claims Inventory.
- Never invent a quote. See Quote Rules.
- Never misrepresent cultural authenticity.
- Never use blogspeak structure: the rhetorical question opener, the "But here's the thing" pivot, the three-word sentence fragment for emphasis. For emphasis. Like this.
- Never write a line that implies the previous product was inferior.
- Never write "authentic." The word is doing a job the facts should be doing. If you feel the need to write it, you have not written the fact yet.

### Handle with care

- **"Made this morning."** True for broth, confirmed on video. **Not confirmed for noodles.** Do not apply the morning claim to noodles until Sam confirms the production cadence. This is the easiest wrong sentence to write on the whole site.
- **Noodle rollout status.** House-made noodles are rolling out to all locations. **Whether every location has switched over is not confirmed.** Write "made in our own Noodle Room," not "every bowl at every location," until Q14 of the interview is answered. The site cannot make a claim the kitchen has not finished delivering.
- **Matsuhisa.** The credential is real. Which location, which years, and what role are all undocumented. Write "worked at Matsuhisa under Nobu Matsuhisa." Do not write a year, a city, or a title.
- **"A few miles away."** Crown Point to Convoy is roughly seven miles, to Plaza Bonita roughly seventeen. "A few miles" is true for the beach locations and false for the South Bay. Prefer "across town," "in Crown Point," or "in our own kitchen." Use a mileage number only if someone measures it.
- **Number of locations.** Six in San Diego. Eight total including Tijuana and Maui. The official site currently contradicts itself. Until it is fixed, write "six San Diego locations" and do not give a global total.

### Never write

- Any founding date other than 2001. Older press says 1994. It is wrong. Do not engage it, do not correct it publicly.
- Anything about the cancelled 2024 to 2025 Japanese corporate acquisition.
- Anything about North Park. It is closed. It does not exist in new copy.
- Carnitas Ramen, or any fusion framing of the brand. Not Japanese-Mexican, not Japanese-Californian, not Baja anything.
- Any suggestion that Tajima was previously less than it is now.
- Competitor names. Fine in internal positioning, never in public copy.
- Employee counts, revenue, or any number nobody has verified.

## Quote Rules

**Sam has not been interviewed yet.** This is the most important constraint on this file.

There are exactly two usable public Sam quotes on record: "Ramen is freedom" (Beyondish, 2026) and the 2020 San Diego Magazine line about not wanting ramen too authentic, **which is off-limits and is the reason the interview matters**.

Therefore:

- **Do not write a Sam quote.** Not a paraphrase in quotation marks, not a "something he might say," not a composite from the video transcript dressed up as speech. A fabricated founder quote on a page whose entire argument is "this is real" is the one mistake that cannot be walked back.
- Facts from the video transcript can be written **as prose in the brand voice**, not as quotes. "Sam spent two years building the room" is fine. "'It took me two years,' Sam says" is not, unless he said exactly that.
- Every page that wants a Sam quote gets a `[QUOTE PENDING INTERVIEW]` marker instead. Those markers are a launch blocker and they are supposed to be.
- Once the interview lands, quotes get pulled from the transcript verbatim, tagged by use, and approved by Sam before publication.

The same rule covers the Noodle Room lead. That person exists, we do not have their name, and we do not write around the gap by inventing a character.

### Preferred terms

| Use | Instead of |

| ramen house | restaurant, eatery, spot, joint |
| the Noodle Room | our noodle facility, the production kitchen |
| house-made | artisanal, handcrafted, homemade |
| broth, simmered | soup, broth base, slow-cooked to perfection |
| tonkotsu | rich pork broth |
| chashu | pork belly, sliced pork |
| bowl | dish, plate, offering |
| the crew | our team members, our staff family |
| Sam | our founder, the owner |
| since 2001 | for over two decades, for 25+ years |
| Amagasaki | Japan |
| Convoy | the Kearny Mesa area |
| walk in | visit us today, come on down |

### The working vocabulary

ramen house, noodle room, broth, simmered, house-made, since 2001, San Diego, Amagasaki, Convoy, tonkotsu, chashu, takenoko, izakaya, karaage, gyoza, Tajima Red / Black / White, slurp, bowl, the master, Sam, crew, the family table, daily, by hand, the room, the machine, flour, dough, steam

### Avoided terms

- **authentic.** Overused into meaninglessness, and Tajima has a specific history with the word. Show it.
- **elevated, curated, immersive.** Restaurant-marketing default settings. They describe nothing.
- **journey.** Sam's path from Amagasaki to Convoy is a real journey. Nothing on this website is a culinary journey.
- **passion.** Everyone claims it. It proves nothing.
- **artisanal, handcrafted.** We have a machine imported from Japan. "Handcrafted" is both off and slightly false. "House-made" is accurate and stronger.
- **fusion.** Never.
- **secret recipe, secret ingredient.** The whole positioning is that we show the work.
- **hidden gem.** Six locations and 25 years. Nothing is hidden.

## Example Content

### Homepage hero

> **Better noodles weren't for sale. So we made them.**
>
> Tajima has been on Convoy Street since 2001. The noodles are made in our own room in Crown Point, on a machine we brought over from Japan. The broth is simmered every morning and driven to all six San Diego locations.
>
> [Find your Tajima] [See the Noodle Room]

Why it works: three sentences, three verifiable facts, zero adjectives doing persuasive work. The reader is told what happens, not how to feel about it.

### Menu item description

**On brand:**
> **Spicy Sesame Ramen**
> Pork bone broth with sesame paste, seasoned ground pork, half a ramen egg, corn, wood ear mushrooms, baby bok choy, fried garlic, dried chili. House-made noodles.

**Off brand:**
> **Spicy Sesame Ramen**
> A truly indulgent bowl that takes your taste buds on a journey. Our signature spicy broth is lovingly crafted with premium sesame paste and topped with an array of curated ingredients for an unforgettable experience.

The menu does not sell. It tells you what is in the bowl. The ingredients are the argument.

### About section

> Sam Morikizono grew up in Amagasaki, near Osaka, eating his mother's cooking. He came to the United States at nineteen and washed dishes at a Japanese restaurant in Rockford, Illinois. He worked his way through kitchens, including a stretch at Matsuhisa under Nobu Matsuhisa, and in 2001 he took over a small Japanese restaurant on Convoy Street.
>
> The restaurant was already called Tajima. He didn't name it. He made it his.
>
> Twenty-five years later there are six of them across San Diego, and the noodles come from his own kitchen.

Every sentence is a fact. No sentence tells the reader what to think about the facts. That is the whole technique.

### Noodle Room page opening

> For twenty-five years, Tajima bought its noodles. They came frozen, from a factory, like almost every ramen shop in America.
>
> Sam spent two years building a room in Crown Point so that would stop being true. There is a machine in it that came over from Japan. It makes two kinds of noodle right now, the ramen noodle and the spinach noodle that goes into the vegan bowl.
>
> The broth is made in the same building, every morning, and driven out to all six locations before service.

Note what this does not do: it does not call the old noodles bad. It says what they were, factually, and then says what changed. The reader does the math. That restraint is the voice.

## Tone by surface

| Surface | Calibration |
|---|---|
| **Home** | Confident, short, front-loaded. The reader decides in six seconds. |
| **Menu** | Flat and factual. Ingredients only. No selling. |
| **Noodle Room** | The most editorial surface on the site. Longer sentences allowed. Still no metaphor. This page has its own visual identity and its copy can breathe with it. |
| **About** | Narrative. The one place the story runs in order. Still short sentences. |
| **Location pages** | Practical and neighborhood-specific. What is here, what is different, how to get in. Convoy is the original. Mercury has the bar and the sushi. East Village is walkable from Petco. Crown Point has the room. Plaza Bonita is quick-serve. Say the real thing. |
| **FAQ** | Answer the question in the first sentence. No preamble. These are written to be quoted by a machine. |
| **Meta and alt text** | Same rules. No em dashes, no filler. Alt text describes the image, it does not sell the image. |

## Client-Specific Context

**The reader who matters most has eaten ramen up and down Convoy.** Kenji, the comparison shopper, is Convoy-literate, skeptical of soft authenticity claims, and will read the whole menu before ordering. Copy that would impress a tourist loses him. Copy that gives him one specific fact he did not have keeps him. When the audience is ambiguous, write for him.

**Plaza Bonita is the exception.** South Bay, family-driven, mall traffic, Spanish-primary. The craft story lands softer there. The food being recognizably itself matters more than the Noodle Room. Location copy adjusts. Brand voice does not.

**This is a sharpening, not a rebrand.** Tajima led with welcome for twenty-five years and it worked. We now lead with craft and the welcome sits underneath. Nothing in the copy suggests a break with the past, a new era, a reinvention, or a relaunch. The brand walks into the next decade more itself, not someone else. If a line sounds like an announcement, it is wrong.

**The Noodle Room has never been covered by press.** Zero mentions of "house-made" or "handmade" appear across 2,775 captured reviews. Nobody knows this yet. That means the copy is not reminding anyone of anything. It is telling them for the first time, and it should read that way: plainly, like news that happens to be true, not like a campaign.
